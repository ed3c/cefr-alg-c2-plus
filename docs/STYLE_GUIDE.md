# English Studio style guide

## Purpose

Help a learner understand one situation, then communicate its meaning accurately. Clear expression is compatible with sophisticated English. Do not replace familiar words with rare synonyms merely to look advanced.

This document guides content authors. It is an adaptation of selected writing principles, not formal ASD-STE100 compliance or a validated Agent behavior policy.

## A learning scene

Give the reader a setting, the people involved, and a concrete reason to care. Each scene changes one part of the situation. Show a decision and its consequence through the conversation. Explain necessary technical terms in context. A reader should not need another chat to know what happened.

The default listening journey asks for no speech, translation, vocabulary recall, or graded answer. Keep transcripts optional. Keep explicit word study and correction in the optional writing workshop. Do not make speaking readiness depend on a invented number of hours or a completion streak.

Create two versions only when they help comprehension. Keep the same actor, material facts, condition, negation, commitment, and uncertainty. The nuanced version may make an implication explicit if it already follows from the shared context. It must not invent a successful outcome or replace a proposal with an observation.

## Written register

Lead with the information the reader needs. Prefer an explicit actor and an active verb. Put a condition before the action it governs when that improves clarity. Keep one term for one technical concept. Explain why a choice follows from the constraint. Include the alternative only when it changes the decision.

Sentence length is a review cue. A sentence over 25 words can be correct and clear. A short sentence can be false or misleading. Never treat a count or a keyword as semantic acceptance.

When a draft is wrong, identify its claim, assumed premise, and actual evidence. Correct the inference as well as the wording. Preserve meaningful uncertainty such as “may,” “not yet,” and “in this test.”

## Spoken register

Keep the same meaning while making the delivery conversational. Contractions are welcome. Short acknowledgments such as “Right” or “Fair point” can show turn-taking. Use a concrete example and allow a natural pause. A polite disagreement should acknowledge the other person's concern without conceding an unsupported claim.

Technical terms may remain exact. Everyday words may vary with the context. Do not apply a controlled-language prohibition on idioms to ordinary conversation. Prefer phrases that a listener can understand from the scene, such as “rule out” or “something to go on.”

Do not force the learner to mimic a model, use a prestige accent, or produce output during listening. A model is one possible response. It is not a required answer.

## Meaning-preserving review

For each paired dialogue and before/after example, check:

1. Who acts? Did the actor change?
2. What is known? Is the evidence tied to the right version or situation?
3. What remains uncertain? Did a qualification disappear?
4. What condition changes the decision?
5. What action was actually promised?
6. Can a reader reconstruct why the choice follows?

Keep fictional scene facts distinct from real product evidence. These scenarios do not report actual incidents in Soodles, medium-compiler, or another repository.

## Worked example

Misleading: “All payment problems have been resolved. The release will be ready tomorrow.”

Faithful: “The sandbox payment test passed. We are still investigating the reported duplicate charge. Maya will send an update by noon tomorrow.”

Spoken: “The sandbox test passed, which is encouraging. But we still haven’t explained the duplicate charge. Let’s investigate that before we commit to the release.”

The revision preserves a narrow passing result and an unresolved problem. The spoken version uses a natural transition while retaining the same uncertainty. Neither example proves the learner has mastered this distinction.

## Evidence boundary

Author review can identify supported content defects. It cannot establish another Agent's behavior or a learner's proficiency. A self-review checkbox records the learner's judgment. Review cues do not issue a pass. CEFR assessment belongs to a separate suitable test or qualified assessment process.
